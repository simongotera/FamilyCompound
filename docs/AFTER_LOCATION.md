# After the location: from "we picked a region" to a living commune

This is the roadmap for everything that happens once the quiz (`/quiz`) has given us a region.
It is deliberately methodical, because the expensive mistakes in this kind of project are all
made early and quietly: the wrong water, the wrong title, the wrong agreement between people
who love each other.

**How to use it.** The phases overlap in time but not in dependency — each phase lists what must
be true before the next one is worth starting. Work the checklists in order. Anything with a
⚖️ needs a local lawyer; anything with a 💧 needs a licensed local professional (hydrologist,
well driller, soil lab, engineer). Nothing in this document is legal, tax or engineering advice,
and none of it is specific to a jurisdiction we haven't picked yet.

**A note on numbers.** This document deliberately contains almost no prices, yields or rainfall
figures. Everything that matters is local, and a plausible-looking number from the wrong place
is worse than no number. Put real figures into the app (Country Profiles, parcels, and the
quiz's Research & data tab) with a source attached, where everyone can see and check them.

**Phases at a glance**

| Phase | What it is | Ends when |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Agreement before land | We know who is in, for what, and on what terms |
| 1 | Land search in the chosen region | We have 3–5 real candidate parcels |
| 2 | Due diligence | One parcel survives water, soil, title, access and zoning |
| 3 | Ownership and legal structure | ⚖️ The entity exists and can hold title |
| 4 | Purchase and first 90 days | We own it, and we have lived on it |
| 5 | Master plan | A permaculture-zoned plan everyone has signed off |
| 6 | Water, power, access | The land can support people year-round |
| 7 | Orchards and food forest | The slow things are in the ground |
| 8 | Housing | People sleep there permanently |
| 9 | Living commune | Governance, work and money run themselves |

## Phase 0 — Agreement before land (do this *before* spending money)

The commonest way intentional communities fail is not money or soil. It is that people never
wrote down what they each expected, and discovered the mismatch after the purchase, when
leaving is expensive. This phase is cheap and uncomfortable; do it anyway.

**Checklist**

- [ ] Write a one-page **shared purpose**: what this place is for, in plain words. Everyone
      writes their own version first, separately, then we merge. Disagreements found here are
      a gift.
- [ ] Each household states, in writing: money they can commit, money they can commit *later*,
      labour they can commit, when they would actually move, and what would make them leave.
- [ ] Decide **who is a member** at purchase versus who is a guest, a future member, or an
      investor. These are three different things and should never be blurred.
- [ ] Agree the **decision rule** for this phase only (see Phase 9 for the long-term version).
      Consent-based ("no one has a reasoned objection") works better than majority vote at
      small sizes, because a 4–3 vote on where the houses go poisons a decade.
- [ ] Agree a **spending ceiling** that one person can commit without a group decision.
- [ ] Agree what happens if someone **wants out** before purchase. Nobody is trapped yet; say
      so explicitly.
- [ ] Write down the **non-negotiables** per household (these are the quiz's dealbreakers —
      log them on the Priorities page so they're in one place).
- [ ] Set a **decision deadline**. Open-ended searches drift for years.

**Exit criteria:** every household has signed a short memorandum of understanding — not legally
binding, deliberately — saying what they expect to put in and get out. ⚖️ Have a lawyer look at
it only to confirm it does *not* accidentally create a partnership or a security.

## Phase 1 — Land search in the chosen region

The quiz narrowed the region. Now we narrow to parcels. Log every candidate on the Land page so
the quiz can score it against the same criteria.

**Checklist**

- [ ] Define the **search box**: maximum drive time to a hospital, to a town with a school and
      a hardware store, to an airport. Draw it on a map; it eliminates half the listings.
- [ ] Set **minimum acreage** from what we actually want to do, not from romance. Count: homes
      and their setbacks, orchards, annual gardens, water storage, access roads, septic or
      treatment fields, and whatever buffer the zoning demands between dwellings.
- [ ] Walk the **aspect and slope** rule: in the northern hemisphere, south-facing slopes get
      more sun and warm earlier; in the southern, north-facing. Cold air pools in valley
      bottoms — frost pockets kill fruit blossom. Mid-slope is usually the orchard.
- [ ] List **every listing source**: local agents, notaries, municipal boards, the "for sale by
      owner" signs that never reach the internet, and word of mouth in the nearest village.
      In most rural markets the best parcels never get listed online.
- [ ] Visit in the **worst season**, not the best. Mud, flood, wind, heat, smoke.
- [ ] Talk to the **neighbours** before making an offer. They will tell you about the water,
      the road in winter, the previous owner, and the plans for the next field over.
- [ ] For each candidate, record in the app: price, acreage, water source, zoning, access,
      utilities at the road, and a source for each claim.

**Exit criteria:** 3–5 parcels that all pass a first look, scored in the quiz, with no
dealbreaker triggered.

## Phase 2 — Due diligence (the phase that saves the project)

Spend real money here. A few thousand on tests and searches is the cheapest insurance in the
whole endeavour. Never waive these to win a bidding war; a parcel you can't water or build on
is not a bargain.

### Water 💧

Water is the single most common fatal flaw, and the hardest to fix after purchase.

- [ ] **Legal right to the water**, separately from the physical water. In some places water
      rights are attached to the land; in others they are owned, traded or rationed separately,
      and a well or a stream on your deed does not mean you may use it. ⚖️
- [ ] **Existing wells**: depth, static level, recovery rate, date drilled, driller's log,
      and whether the permit transfers.
- [ ] **A pump test** on any well we intend to rely on, long enough to show recovery, done by
      a licensed driller. A well that gives a strong first hour and nothing after is common.
- [ ] **Water quality test** for the local risks — at minimum bacteriological, nitrates, arsenic
      and heavy metals; ask the local lab what else this area is known for.
- [ ] **Surface water**: streams, springs and ponds, their seasonality, and the right to dam,
      divert or store.
- [ ] **Rainwater**: annual total, and its distribution. The same yearly rainfall split as six
      wet months and six dry is a completely different design problem than spread evenly.
- [ ] **Neighbouring demand**: a big irrigator or an industry upstream or up-aquifer changes
      our future even if our paperwork is perfect.

### Soil 💧

- [ ] **Laboratory soil tests** from several spots and depths, not one. Request texture,
      organic matter, pH, CEC, macro and micronutrients.
- [ ] **Contamination screen** where history warrants: old orchards (lead arsenate), vineyards
      (copper), fuel tanks, dumping, mine tailings, or any industrial neighbour.
- [ ] **Dig holes**. A soil pit tells you about depth to rock, hardpan, and the water table in
      a way no map does.
- [ ] **Drainage test** (a simple percolation test) where buildings and septic might go.
- [ ] **Local knowledge**: what grows well on this soil within ten kilometres, and what fails.

### Title, boundaries and access ⚖️

- [ ] **Full title search** by a lawyer or notary we hired — never only the seller's.
- [ ] **Liens, mortgages, unpaid taxes, inheritance claims.** Rural land in many countries has
      tangled inheritance: confirm every heir has signed.
- [ ] **Easements and rights of way**, both ours over others' land and others' over ours:
      utility corridors, grazing rights, hunting rights, traditional paths, logging access.
- [ ] **A real survey** with marked corners. Fences are not boundaries.
- [ ] **Legal access**: a deeded, year-round right to reach the parcel from a public road. A
      track across a neighbour's field "that nobody minds" is not access.
- [ ] **Mineral, timber and water rights** — whether they were severed from the surface rights.
- [ ] **Foreign-ownership rules**: restrictions near borders or coasts, registration
      requirements, and whether an entity or a person must hold the title. ⚖️

### Zoning, permits and what we may build ⚖️

This is where the multi-household dream most often meets a wall. Ask the planning office
directly, in writing, before buying.

- [ ] **How many dwellings** are allowed on this parcel as it stands, and under what conditions.
- [ ] Whether **subdivision** is possible, how long it takes, and what it costs.
- [ ] The status of **accessory dwellings, cabins, tiny homes, yurts, and RVs**, and whether
      "temporary" occupancy while building is permitted and for how long.
- [ ] **Septic / wastewater** rules and whether the soil passes for the number of people planned.
- [ ] **Building codes**: whether owner-building is permitted, and the status of natural
      building methods if we want them (strawbale, cob, rammed earth, timber frame).
- [ ] **Agricultural classification**: it often lowers property tax dramatically but carries
      use requirements and clawbacks if we stop farming.
- [ ] **Protected status**: conservation overlays, heritage, protected species, flood zones,
      wildfire interface rules, forestry restrictions.
- [ ] **Hazard mapping**: official flood, fire, landslide and seismic maps, plus what the
      insurer says — insurability is a good independent read on risk.

### Climate and hazard reality check

- [ ] **Frost dates and growing-degree days** from the nearest real weather station, not a
      national average.
- [ ] **Microclimate**: this parcel's own frost pockets, wind exposure, and sun hours in winter.
- [ ] **Trend, not just history**: how the last 20 years compare to the 30-year normals for
      rainfall, extreme heat and fire season length.

**Exit criteria:** one parcel with no unresolved red flag, a written summary of every finding
with its source in the app, and a price we are willing to pay knowing all of it. Log the
decision and the reasoning on the Decisions page.

## Phase 3 — Ownership and legal structure ⚖️

**Everything in this section is an orientation for the conversation with a lawyer, not a
recommendation.** Names and behaviour of these structures differ completely between countries;
some do not exist outside the US. Hire a lawyer in the jurisdiction, and a tax adviser in each
country where a member is tax-resident, before choosing.

Decide the structure *before* the purchase if you can. Retitling land later can trigger transfer
tax a second time.

### The main families of structure

**Tenancy in common (co-owners on one deed)**
Simple and cheap to start. Each owner holds a share; shares can be unequal.
*Pros:* minimal setup, familiar to any notary.
*Cons:* any co-owner may usually force a sale of the whole property through a partition action
— one person's divorce or death can unwind the project; financing is awkward; no built-in
governance. Use only with a strong written co-ownership agreement, and understand that in some
jurisdictions a waiver of partition rights is unenforceable. ⚖️

**A company (LLC, SARL, SL, Ltda, GmbH — the local equivalent)**
The entity owns the land; members own the entity.
*Pros:* governance lives in the operating agreement, which can be as detailed as we like;
transfers of membership are cleaner than transfers of land; liability separation; familiar to
banks.
*Cons:* annual filings and cost; may lose agricultural tax treatment in some places; foreign
members can create reporting burdens and tax surprises; an entity that looks like an investment
vehicle can attract securities regulation if members are passive. ⚖️

**Cooperative**
A member-owned legal form that exists in most countries, often with its own statute, sometimes
with housing-specific variants (a housing co-op where members hold a right to occupy rather
than a deed).
*Pros:* built for one-member-one-vote governance; strong cultural fit with a commune; some
jurisdictions offer favourable treatment or financing.
*Cons:* statutory rigidity — the law may dictate governance details we'd rather choose; more
formal record-keeping; harder to explain to a lender; in some countries membership transfers
are tightly regulated.

**Land trust (private, family)**
A trustee holds title for named beneficiaries.
*Pros:* privacy, straightforward succession across generations, can keep land out of probate.
*Cons:* "trust" in a common-law sense does not exist in many civil-law countries, where a
foundation or a usufruct arrangement is the nearest analogue; trustee selection and succession
is a governance problem of its own; not by itself a governance structure for people who live
together. ⚖️

**Community land trust (CLT)**
A non-profit holds the land permanently; residents own or lease the buildings and hold a
long-term ground lease, typically with a resale formula that keeps homes affordable.
*Pros:* keeps the land out of the speculative market forever; strong anti-displacement and
mission-lock properties; aligns well with the stewardship-not-ownership idea.
*Cons:* members do not capture land appreciation — that is the point, and it must be understood
and genuinely wanted by everyone, including heirs; significant setup and ongoing administration;
often needs a board with outside members; financing homes on leased land is harder.

**Hybrids** are common and often right: e.g. a CLT or trust holding the land, with a company or
co-op operating the farm, plus leases for individual home sites.

### Questions to take to the lawyer

- How is the land held, and what happens to it if the entity dissolves?
- How does a member **join**? What do they pay, and how is that price set?
- How does a member **leave**? What are they owed, how is it valued, who pays, over what period?
  (Forcing a sale of land to pay out one exiting member is the failure mode to design against.)
- What happens on **death, divorce, bankruptcy or incapacity** of a member?
- Can a member's share be **inherited**? By anyone, or only by someone the group accepts?
- Who may **live** here, as against who **owns**? Partners, children, friends, tenants?
- Who can **encumber** the land — can one member borrow against it?
- How is **decision-making** recorded so it is legally effective?
- What **taxes** apply: transfer tax at purchase, annual property tax, income tax on farm
  revenue, VAT on sales, capital gains at exit, and the cross-border treatment for each member?
- What **insurance** is required, and what does it exclude (fire, flood, liability for guests
  and volunteers)?
- Does any of this create a **regulated security** by selling shares to people who are not
  living and working here? ⚖️

**Exit criteria:** an entity that exists, has a bank account, can hold title, and a founding
agreement that every member has read in a language they are fluent in.

## Phase 4 — Purchase and the first 90 days

### Buying

- [ ] Offer conditional on due diligence still outstanding (a financing condition, a survey
      condition, a water-test condition — whatever is not yet finished).
- [ ] Our own lawyer or notary, never the seller's, however friendly.
- [ ] Funds moved only through the lawyer's or notary's escrow account, and wire details
      confirmed by voice on a number we looked up ourselves. Property-purchase wire fraud is
      common and unrecoverable.
- [ ] Register the title and confirm registration afterwards — don't assume it happened.
- [ ] Insurance active from the day of transfer.

### The first 90 days — do less than you want to

The temptation is to start building. Resist it. The first season is for learning the land, and
cheap observation now prevents expensive mistakes later. The one exception is anything that
takes years to mature (nursery stock, soil building, water catchment), which starts now.

**Days 1–30: observe and secure**

- [ ] Walk every boundary; confirm the survey markers are where the survey says.
- [ ] Secure the site: gate, locks, a sign, and a conversation with the neighbours.
- [ ] Set up a **rain gauge, a min/max thermometer and a few soil thermometers**, and start a
      log. Our own year of data beats any regional average.
- [ ] Map, by walking, where water goes when it rains hard. Photograph it. This map determines
      everything in Phase 5.
- [ ] Note where the sun actually rises and sets, what is shaded in winter, where the wind comes
      from, where frost lingers in the morning.
- [ ] Find the local: lumber yard, machinery hire, soil lab, extension service or agricultural
      office, large-animal vet, well driller, excavator operator, and the person everyone says
      knows this valley.

**Days 31–60: minimal infrastructure**

- [ ] Reliable water for people and for watering young plants.
- [ ] Somewhere dry and lockable to keep tools.
- [ ] A legal place to sleep and a legal toilet, even if temporary — confirm with the planning
      office what is permitted and for how long.
- [ ] Road and gate passable for a delivery truck.
- [ ] Begin **soil building** on the areas earmarked for orchard: cover crops, mulch, compost.
      Soil takes seasons; start it before you need it.
- [ ] Order or propagate **nursery stock** for the first orchard planting window.

**Days 61–90: plan with evidence**

- [ ] Commission a contour or topographic survey if the design needs one (it usually does).
- [ ] Draft the master plan (Phase 5) using the water map and the sun observations.
- [ ] Hold the first full governance meeting on the land itself.
- [ ] Write down what surprised us. Revisit the Phase 0 agreement in light of it.

## Phase 5 — Master planning with permaculture zones

Plan on paper for a year before moving earth. Design from patterns to details: water first, then
access, then structures, then planting. Where roads and water go determines everything else, and
they are the hardest things to move later.

**The zone model** (a planning heuristic about frequency of visits, not a rulebook):

- **Zone 0** — the dwellings themselves.
- **Zone 1** — what you touch daily: kitchen garden, herbs, salad, propagation, washing line.
  Immediately outside the door.
- **Zone 2** — several times a week: intensive orchard, soft fruit, chickens, compost, bees,
  small ponds.
- **Zone 3** — weekly to seasonal: main crops, the big orchard, pasture, larger water storage.
- **Zone 4** — occasional: grazing, timber, coppice, forage, wind shelter.
- **Zone 5** — unmanaged. Left alone on purpose, as a reference ecosystem and as a place to
  learn from. Decide its boundary early, before every acre has a job.

**Sector analysis** — the forces that come *from outside* and that siting must respond to:
sun path (summer and winter), prevailing and storm winds, water flow and flood lines, fire
approach (wildfire runs uphill and with the wind — keep the defensible space on that side),
frost pockets, noise, dust, headlights, views you want and views you don't, and the neighbours'
spray drift.

**Checklist**

- [ ] Base map with contours, existing trees, water, buildings, access and boundaries.
- [ ] Overlay the sector analysis.
- [ ] Site **water storage first** — high in the landscape where possible, so gravity does the
      distribution work for free.
- [ ] Then **access roads and paths**, following contour where possible; roads double as water
      management and firebreaks.
- [ ] Then **house sites**: sun, drainage, slope, distance to shared infrastructure, privacy
      between households, and the firebreak. Plan where every future household will go, now,
      even for households that don't exist yet. Retrofitting a neighbour into a finished plan
      is a governance crisis waiting to happen.
- [ ] Then **shared buildings**: the commons kitchen/hall, workshop, barn, processing space.
- [ ] Then **planting zones**, orchard blocks and windbreaks.
- [ ] Mark **phase boundaries** on the plan: what happens in year 1, 3, 5, 10.
- [ ] Mark what is **private** and what is **common** — physically, on the map. Ambiguity here
      becomes conflict later.
- [ ] Walk the plan on the ground with flags and string before anyone digs.

**Exit criteria:** a plan every household has walked, marked up and consented to, with the
private/common boundary explicit. (The proposed visual site planner — see
[SITE_PLANNER_PLAN.md](SITE_PLANNER_PLAN.md) — is meant to make exactly this step collaborative
rather than one person with a pen.)

## Phase 6 — Water, energy and the systems that make it habitable

### Water

- [ ] **Catchment and storage** sized for the dry season, not the average: roof catchment, swales
      and infiltration where legal, ponds and tanks. Check the legality of rainwater harvesting
      and of damming — it is restricted in some jurisdictions. ⚖️
- [ ] **Gravity before pumps.** Every metre of head is free energy and one less thing to break.
- [ ] **Separate potable from irrigation** supply, and plumb them so they cannot cross-connect.
- [ ] **Greywater** reuse where legal, designed with the soil's percolation in mind.
- [ ] **Wastewater**: septic, constructed wetland, or composting toilets — whatever is both
      legal and appropriate; get it engineered and permitted.
- [ ] **Redundancy**: at least two independent sources before anyone depends on the place
      year-round.

### Energy

- [ ] **Reduce demand first.** Insulation, orientation, shading, efficient appliances and a
      wood or solar hot-water strategy are cheaper per unit than generating more.
- [ ] **Grid-tied, hybrid or off-grid** — decide deliberately. Off-grid is a lifestyle and a
      maintenance commitment, not only a cost; a grid connection at the road may be cheaper than
      the battery bank it replaces. Hybrid with battery backup is often the honest answer.
- [ ] **Size for the worst month**, usually midwinter, not the annual average.
- [ ] **Shared or per-household** generation? Shared is cheaper per watt and creates a
      metering-and-fairness question; per-household is simpler socially. Decide with Phase 9's
      governance, not purely on cost.
- [ ] **Cooking and heating fuel**: wood (and whether the land can sustainably supply it),
      bottled gas, electric. Wood needs a coppice plan started early.
- [ ] **Backup** that does not depend on the internet or a vendor's cloud service.

### Connectivity, roads and fire

- [ ] Internet that can carry remote work, with a backup path.
- [ ] All-weather access for an ambulance and a fire truck, including turning space.
- [ ] **Wildfire**: defensible space around every structure, water available for firefighting,
      a written evacuation plan, and ember-resistant detailing if we're in a fire region.
- [ ] Address, signage and emergency-service registration so help can find us.

## Phase 7 — Orchards and the food forest (start early, these are the slow ones)

Simon's family's orchards are the reason for the project, and trees are the one thing that
cannot be hurried. A fruit tree planted in year 1 begins to crop in roughly years 3–6 depending
on species and rootstock; some nuts take longer. **Plant before you build.** Every year of delay
is a year of fruit you never get.

### Before planting

- [ ] Confirm the **chill hours / growing zone** for the site and choose varieties that match.
      This is the commonest planting mistake: beautiful trees that never fruit because the
      winter is too warm or the late frost takes the blossom every year.
- [ ] Choose **rootstocks** deliberately — they determine final size, soil tolerance, disease
      resistance and how soon it crops, often more than the variety does.
- [ ] Check **pollination partners**: many fruits need a compatible second variety flowering at
      the same time. Design the blocks with this in mind.
- [ ] Buy from a **local or regional nursery** where possible: better-adapted stock, and less
      risk of importing disease. Check import rules for any plant material crossing a border.
- [ ] Prepare the soil the season **before**: cover crop, compost, mineral amendments the soil
      test actually asked for, and deep decompaction if there's a pan.
- [ ] Plan **windbreaks first** — they take years too, and young trees need them.
- [ ] Plan **browse protection**: deer, livestock, rabbits, voles. More young orchards are lost
      to animals than to weather.

### A sequence that works

**Year 0 (the first planting window):** windbreak species, nitrogen-fixing support species,
and the longest-to-fruit trees (nuts, standard-rootstock apples, chestnuts). Mulch heavily.
Water plan in place before the trees arrive, not after.

**Year 1:** the main orchard blocks — stone fruit, pome fruit on semi-dwarfing rootstock.
Soft fruit and berries along the edges (they crop in year 2 and keep morale up). Formative
pruning begins.

**Year 2:** fill the understory — the food-forest layers: support shrubs, herbs, perennial
vegetables, ground cover, climbers. Establish the pollinator and beneficial-insect plantings.

**Year 3:** first meaningful harvests from soft fruit and the earliest trees. Start the
propagation nursery so future planting is free. Review what died and why, honestly.

**Years 4–7:** main orchard comes into bearing. Processing becomes the bottleneck — plan storage,
drying, pressing, canning and cold storage *before* the glut, not during it.

**Ongoing:** a written annual calendar — pruning windows, grafting, pest monitoring, harvest,
and succession planting to replace trees as they age.

### Record-keeping

- [ ] Map every tree: species, variety, rootstock, source, planting date.
- [ ] Log what fruits, what fails, what gets sick. Over a decade this becomes the most valuable
      document on the property, and it is the thing nobody remembers to start.

## Phase 8 — Housing, in phases

- [ ] **Temporary legal housing first** — confirm what is permitted and for how long. Living on
      site while building is a huge advantage and often restricted.
- [ ] **Shared infrastructure before private comfort**: water, power, sanitation, and the
      commons kitchen serve everyone and should be built first.
- [ ] **Build one house well before building four.** The first build teaches the lessons; pay
      for them once.
- [ ] Decide **build vs contract** per element honestly. Owner-building saves money and costs
      time, marriages and sometimes permits; some elements (foundations, electrics, septic,
      structure) are worth paying professionals for whatever our skills.
- [ ] **Design for ageing and for guests.** Single-level access on at least one dwelling;
      somewhere for family to stay that isn't someone's sofa.
- [ ] **Sound and sight privacy** between households matters more than anyone expects and is
      nearly free at design time.
- [ ] **Sequence**: commons → first dwelling → second dwelling → workshop/barn → the rest, with
      each one's budget and permit secured before starting it.

## Phase 9 — Governance, money and the living commune

The land will be fine. The hard part is the people, and it stays the hard part forever. These
agreements should exist in writing *before* anyone moves in, and should be scheduled for
periodic review, because the version written by four adults is wrong once there are children,
elders, partners and guests.

### Decision-making

- [ ] Choose the **decision method** and write it down: consent (no reasoned objection),
      consensus, supermajority, or delegated domains. A workable default: delegate day-to-day
      decisions to whoever holds that domain, and reserve money over a threshold, membership,
      and land use for the whole group.
- [ ] Define **domains** and who holds them (water, orchard, buildings, finances, hospitality).
      Rotate them; a rotation prevents both burnout and quiet power.
- [ ] Set a **meeting rhythm** — a short weekly operational one and a longer monthly or seasonal
      one — with an agenda anyone can add to, and written minutes. Keep decisions on the
      Decisions page so they are findable years later.
- [ ] Agree a **quorum** and what happens when someone is away.
- [ ] Define which decisions **cannot** be made without unanimity (selling land, taking on debt,
      admitting or removing a member).

### Money

- [ ] Separate **capital** (who paid for what, and what that entitles them to) from **operating
      costs** (who pays the annual bills) from **labour** (who does the work). Conflating them
      is the classic source of resentment.
- [ ] Decide how **unequal contributions** are handled: equal shares regardless, shares
      proportional to capital, sweat equity converted at an agreed rate, or a loan from the
      member who paid more. Write the formula down and work through two or three real examples
      before adopting it.
- [ ] Budget the **annual carrying cost** — taxes, insurance, maintenance, fuel, repairs — and
      agree who pays it and what happens if someone can't that year.
- [ ] Keep a **reserve fund**. Roofs, pumps, wells and vehicles fail on their own schedule.
- [ ] Decide whether the place is meant to **earn**, and if so from what (fruit, value-added
      products, hosting, education, rentals), who does that work, and who the income belongs to.
      Many communities are happier when the land is not required to be profitable.
- [ ] Track it where everyone can see it — the Budget page is for this.

### Membership, exit and conflict

- [ ] A **joining process** with a trial period. Everyone should be able to say no, including
      the newcomer, without it being a rejection of a person.
- [ ] An **exit formula** that does not force a land sale: a buyout period of years, a valuation
      method agreed in advance (independent appraisal, formula, or a capped appreciation), and
      a right of first refusal for remaining members. ⚖️
- [ ] **Inheritance**: can a share pass to children automatically, or must the heir be accepted?
      What happens if they don't want it?
- [ ] A written **conflict process** with escalating steps: direct conversation first; then a
      third member as a facilitator; then an agreed outside mediator, named in advance while
      everyone is still friends. Agree who pays for mediation now, not during the fight.
- [ ] **Grounds and process for removing a member**, however unthinkable it seems today. ⚖️
- [ ] A **guests and volunteers** policy: how long, whose responsibility, what work is expected,
      insurance, and what happens if they want to stay.
- [ ] A **children and elders** plan: schooling, care, and who is responsible for what.
- [ ] Agree to **review these agreements** on a fixed schedule (annually, say) rather than only
      in a crisis.

### Culture

- [ ] Shared meals on a fixed rhythm. Nearly every long-lived community has this; almost none
      of the failed ones did.
- [ ] A work rhythm that distinguishes **required shared labour** from voluntary projects, with
      the expected hours written down.
- [ ] Protected rest. Idealistic projects burn people out, and burnout reads as conflict.
- [ ] Celebrate the seasons — first fruit, harvest, planting. The orchard provides the calendar.

## Timeline sketch

Highly dependent on money, time and jurisdiction. Shown as a shape, not a promise.

| Period | Focus |
|---|---|
| Months 0–3 | Phase 0 agreements; begin land search |
| Months 3–9 | Search and visits; due diligence on finalists |
| Months 6–12 | Legal structure in place; purchase |
| Year 1 | First 90 days; observation; soil building; **first trees in the ground**; master plan |
| Year 1–2 | Water and access infrastructure; temporary housing; main orchard planting |
| Year 2–4 | Commons building; first permanent dwelling; food-forest understory |
| Year 3–6 | Further dwellings; first real harvests; processing and storage |
| Year 5–10 | Orchards in full bearing; governance matured; the place runs itself |

---

## Red flags — stop and reconsider

- Water that nobody can document, or a seller who discourages a pump test.
- A title the seller's lawyer is in a hurry about, or an heir who hasn't signed.
- "You can definitely build that" said verbally by anyone, including an official. Get it written.
- A parcel that only works if the zoning changes.
- A member who won't put their expectations in writing.
- A group decision that one person is clearly swallowing rather than consenting to.
- A plan whose budget has no reserve.
- Buying before Phase 0 is finished, because the parcel is "too good to lose". There is always
  another parcel; there is not always another family.

## Keeping this in the app

- **Land page** — every candidate parcel and its due-diligence facts.
- **Countries page** — country-level research, with sources.
- **Quiz** (`/quiz`) — priorities, weights, dealbreakers, the ranked shortlist and what would
  flip it. Research & data is where sourced facts live.
- **Priorities page** — each person's must-haves and dealbreakers in their own words.
- **Budget page** — who has committed what.
- **Decisions page** — what we decided and *why*. Future us will need the why.
- **Tasks page** — the checklists above, as they become live.
